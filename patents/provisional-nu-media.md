## Title

Constant-bitrate media transmission with receiver-selected neural enhancement outside the prediction loop, progressive images in constant-rate cover traffic, and private chunked transfer with optional native-resolution transcode

## Cross-reference to related applications

None.

## Field

The disclosure relates to the coding and transmission of images and video, and in particular to media formats and transport for networks that hide traffic patterns by sending fixed-size packets on fixed schedules, to receiver-side neural processing of decoded video, and to the private transfer of large files.

## Background

**Conventional video coding.** HEVC (ITU-T H.265) and its predecessors code video with motion-compensated prediction from reference pictures. Encoder and decoder must reconstruct identical reference pictures, so every step inside the prediction loop must be bit-exact; any mismatch accumulates ("drift"). Scalable extensions (SHVC) and enhancement codecs such as MPEG-5 LCEVC (ISO/IEC 23094-2) add enhancement layers on top of a base codec, but those enhancement layers are themselves coded bits that the sender must transmit.

**Learned codecs.** Learned image codecs such as HiFiC (Mentzer et al., NeurIPS 2020) and ELIC (He et al., CVPR 2022), and learned video codecs of the DCVC family (Li et al., NeurIPS 2021, and later DCVC-DC, DCVC-FM and DCVC-RT), report substantially fewer bits than conventional codecs at equal quality. HiFiC uses a generative adversarial decoder that can synthesise texture not present in the source. Learned codecs that compute their entropy model in floating point can fail to decode when encoder and decoder hardware differ, because tiny numeric differences change the probability tables. Real-time learned video coding has been reported only on data-centre GPUs, not on phones.

**Neural super resolution and interpolation.** Receiver-side super resolution for streaming has been studied (for example NAS, Yeo et al., OSDI 2018, which sends per-video models with the stream; NEMO and LiveNAS for mobile and live video), and is offered in consumer products (for example browser video upscaling on desktop GPUs). Apple VideoToolbox provides a low-latency super-resolution scaler (`VTLowLatencySuperResolutionScaler`) and low-latency frame interpolation running on the Neural Engine. Televisions and games interpolate frames at the display. These systems treat enhancement as a post-process of an ordinary stream and are not designed with transport whose bit budget is fixed for privacy.

**Traffic analysis and constant-rate transport.** Variable-bitrate encrypted media leaks content: packet sizes of encrypted voice calls reveal spoken phrases (Wright et al., IEEE S&P 2008). Mix networks such as Loopix (Piotrowska et al., USENIX Security 2017) and Nym hide who communicates with whom by sending fixed-size packets at fixed or Poisson schedules with cover traffic. Such networks give each user only a small fixed number of bits per unit time (for example a few kilobytes every two seconds), which conventional image and video formats use poorly: a full-screen JPEG or HEIC photo takes many minutes to arrive, and video is impractical.

**Bulk transfer.** IBM Aspera's FASP protocol moves files at close to line rate over long, lossy paths, encrypting them in transit; end-to-end encryption at rest is optional, and the server operator sees file names, sizes and endpoints. Cloud storage services such as Dropbox store files on their servers and by default hold the keys. Systems such as Tahoe-LAFS store encrypted, erasure-coded shares on multiple servers; content-hash-keyed stores (Freenet/Hyphanet) use convergent encryption, which allows confirmation-of-file attacks. Padding schemes such as Padmé (Nikitin et al., PETS 2019) limit size leakage. None of these combines fixed-size, end-to-end sealed chunks pulled by a receiver through constant-rate anonymity carriers with an optional native-resolution transcode chosen by the sender.

**Resolution.** Media sharing services and messengers commonly downscale photos and video on upload. Large 360° and cinema formats (11K to 18K and beyond) exceed the single-picture limits of hardware decoders; viewport-adaptive tiled streaming (for example MPEG OMAF and tiled DASH) addresses delivery over ordinary networks.

## Summary

The disclosure fixes the bits on the wire and moves quality improvement to the receiver.

A sender encodes a video base layer with a hardware encoder at constant bitrate, divides it into fixed-size objects sent at a fixed rate for the life of a session, and fills empty slots with padding objects encrypted so they are indistinguishable from media and of the same ciphertext length. The receiver decodes the base layer and applies neural enhancement (super resolution, artefact removal, chroma restoration, frame interpolation) that it selects for its own hardware. The enhancement has no transmitted bits, is never written into any reference picture, and therefore cannot drift; different receivers of the same stream may enhance it differently. An optional residual layer inside the prediction loop is decoded with integer-only arithmetic so that it is bit-exact on every device, and is dropped by devices that cannot run it.

Images are coded with a learned codec whose entropy model is integer-only, into progressive layers in which the first layer is a preview of the whole image and each later layer refines the whole image. The layers are sized to the fixed packets of an anonymity network's cover traffic and sent in place of cover, so the preview arrives in the first packet.

Media is never downscaled by the sender for storage. Large frames, up to 43,008 × 21,504 pixels, are coded as independent tiles that any hardware decoder can decode, with an overview track and viewport-first fetching. Frames carry 1 MHz timestamps without a frame-rate field, so coded rates up to 2,000 frames per second are possible and receivers interpolate display rates above the coded rate at no bit cost while presenting every coded frame.

Files and stored media are transferred as fixed-size, end-to-end sealed chunks addressed by the hash of their ciphertext, with the chunk count padded, erasure coded in stripes, listed in an encrypted manifest signed with a post-quantum signature, stored on independent nodes, and pulled by the recipient in its chosen order through constant-rate carriers. Before sending, the sender may choose a bit-exact send or a transcode of video to the constant-bitrate format at native resolution, which reduces the bytes moved.

## Detailed description

Numbers are examples; the embodiments apply to other sizes, rates, codecs and networks. Values described as design values have not been measured. Values described as prototype measurements were measured on prototype software and hardware as stated in the Examples.

### 1. System overview (FIG. 1)

Referring to FIG. 1, a sending device 10 (for example a phone, tablet or computer) has a camera or other media source 11, a hardware video encoder 12, a neural accelerator 14, an object framer and encryptor 16 that forms and encrypts fixed-size objects, and a transfer module 18 for files and stored media. A receiving device 20 has a decryptor and reassembler 21, a hardware video decoder 22, a neural accelerator 24 that performs enhancement, display buffers 26 and a display 28.

The devices exchange media through one of several constant-rate carriers 30: (a) foreground "ticks" 32 of an anonymity network of mix nodes 33, in which each device sends a fixed number of fixed-size packets at fixed intervals (for example 3 outgoing packets with 2,048-byte payloads every 2 seconds), filling unused packets with cover; (b) constant-rate call sessions 34 through two relays 35a and 35b operated by different operators and joined by a padded trunk 36; and (c) constant streams 38 of larger packets (for example 65,536-byte payloads) that run from the user's own node 39, from the phone while it is on Wi-Fi and charging, or over call trunks. Each carrier sends the same pattern whether or not media is present. Storage nodes 40 operated by independent operators hold sealed chunks of stored media and files (section 6).

### 2. Constant-bitrate base layer with receiver-only enhancement (FIG. 2, FIG. 3)

**2.1 Layers.** As shown in FIG. 2, a source video 50 is coded by a base encoder 52 (the hardware encoder 12) whose prediction uses its own reference pictures 53, and optionally by a residual encoder 54 that uses integer arithmetic only and keeps its own residual reference 55. The sender emits a base track 56, an optional residual track 57 and an optional enhancement hint 58 that carries no picture data. All of these travel as fixed-size encrypted objects at a fixed rate 59. At the receiver, a base decoder 60 (the hardware decoder 22) and its reference pictures 62 form the base prediction loop 64. A residual decoder 66, the previous residual reconstruction 67 and an integer upsampler 68 form a separate residual loop 69. An enhancement stage 70 on the neural accelerator 24 lies outside both loops and comprises super resolution 71, artefact removal 72, chroma restoration 73, tile seam smoothing 74 and frame interpolation 75, chosen by a selector 76. The enhanced pictures go to the display buffers 26 and the display 28. There is no path 78 from the enhancement stage 70 back to the reference pictures 62 or to the residual loop 69.

**2.2 Tiers.** A session uses one of a ladder of tiers, each defining a fixed object size and object rate, for example 1,200-byte objects at 60, 160 or 400 objects per second (about 576,000, 1,536,000 and 3,840,000 bit/s on the wire), plus an audio track of 160-byte objects at 50 per second carrying constant-bitrate Opus.

**2.3 Base encoding.** The base encoder 52 codes the base layer in HEVC Main 10 (or H.264 on devices without it) at constant bitrate, set to a fraction (for example 0.95) of the tier's payload capacity after encryption and object headers (for example about 524,000, 1,397,000 and 3,493,000 bit/s). In live sessions the encoder uses no frame reordering and periodic intra refresh instead of intra pictures, so coded frame sizes have no periodic peaks, and keeps its coded picture buffer short (for example 100 ms).

**2.4 Objects and padding (FIG. 3).** Objects 82 are sent in slots on a time axis 80 at a fixed interval 81 (T), and every object has the same size on the wire. After decryption some objects are media objects 82m and others are padding objects 82p. Each object comprises an encryption header 84 (for example an SFrame header) and an encrypted portion 86 holding an object header 88, frame data 90 and zero padding 92, followed by an authentication tag 94. The object header 88 carries the object kind, flags for frame start, frame end and random access, the data length, the frame sequence number, a 1 MHz presentation timestamp and a tile index. Every object is encrypted (for example with SFrame and AES-256-GCM) using per-sender keys derived from an MLS group exporter. The sender computes the length of the zero padding 92 after determining the actual length of the encryption header 84, so every ciphertext is exactly the object size (for example 1,200 bytes) even as key identifiers and counters grow. Slots with no media carry padding objects 82p. Objects are sent at exact intervals with no bursts, and each transport packet 96 carrying them is padded with packet padding 98 to one fixed size per session, so variable transport framing does not leak. Tier changes occur rarely (for example at most every 30 s), only between adjacent tiers and only at a random-access point.

**2.5 Enhancement outside the loop.** The receiver decodes the base layer with its base decoder 60 and passes the decoded pictures to one or more neural networks of the enhancement stage 70. The enhanced pictures are written only to the display buffers 26, never to the reference pictures 62 or the residual loop 69. The enhancement therefore needs no transmitted bits, need not be bit-exact, and cannot cause drift. The selector 76 chooses which networks to run and at what size and rate according to the class of the receiver's neural accelerator 24 (for example among chip generations with 8 to 32 neural cores), its thermal state, its battery, the resolution and refresh rate of the display 28, and the hint 58, and may change the selection during a session. Two receivers of one stream may apply different enhancement. A design value for the per-frame budget on a current phone is 8 ms per frame at 60 fps and 300 mW.

**2.6 Hints.** The enhancement hint 58 is a bitmask in the track or frame header that permits or forbids each enhancement operation (for example forbidding interpolation for scrolling text or slides, or forbidding all enhancement for a lossless track) and a ceiling on the display rate; the receiver honours cleared bits. Enhancement networks are part of the receiving application, identified by hash, and are never downloaded in response to a stream, because such a download would reveal what is being watched.

**2.7 Fidelity.** In the preferred embodiment the enhancement networks are trained with fidelity losses against the sender's own base layer output and use no generative adversarial or diffusion synthesis, so they do not invent texture. A general-purpose super-resolution scaler (the one used in the Examples) ran at about 360 frames per second for a 360 × 640 to 720 × 1280 pass on a current phone and improved on a Lanczos upscale of the same reduced base (Example 1), but such general-purpose scalers can add texture that is not in the source; training on the base layer with a fidelity loss addresses this.

**2.8 Reduced coded size.** At low tiers the sender may code the base at a reduced size (for example 960 × 540 for a 1920 × 1080 camera, or 360 × 640 for a 720 × 1280 source), signalled as coded size alongside the native size in the track header, and the receiver's super resolution 71 restores the native or display size. At higher tiers the sender codes at full size. The sender selects the coded size per tier from measured rate-quality curves for the source size and the receiver class; in Example 1 a half-size base with super resolution scored higher than a full-size base at equal bits up to about 700 kbit/s, and the full-size base scored higher at about 1,400 kbit/s. Coding a half-size base also halves the encoder's pixel rate (Example 2).

**2.9 Display size above the coded and native size.** Because enhancement carries no bits, the receiver may present at a size larger than the native size (for example a 720 × 1280 stream on a 2160 × 3840 display) without any change to the transmitted stream (Example 3).

**2.10 Residual layer inside the loop.** Optionally, the residual track 57 carries a learned residual per frame. The residual reconstruction is R_t = clip(U(B_t) + D(r_t, R_(t−1))), where B_t is the decoded base picture, U the integer upsampler 68, r_t the entropy-decoded residual symbols and D a synthesis network of the residual decoder 66 that also takes the previous residual reconstruction 67. D and the residual entropy model use only integer arithmetic (for example INT8 weights, INT32 accumulators, requantisation by arithmetic right shift with round-half-up, saturation to INT16 activations), so every conforming decoder computes identical R_t. At refresh points R_(t−1) is replaced by U(B_t), bounding the effect of any loss. The base layer never references the residual layer, so a receiver that lacks the model or the speed drops the residual and continues with base plus enhancement. The residual may raise output to 12 bits over a 10-bit base.

**2.11 Group sessions.** In sessions with more than two participants, each sender periodically publishes a manifest signed with a post-quantum signature (for example ML-DSA-87) over the authentication tags 94 of all its objects in an interval, padding objects included, so a relay cannot drop objects selectively without detection.

### 3. Progressive learned images inside constant-rate cover (FIG. 4)

**3.1 Codec.** Referring to FIG. 4, an image 100 is coded by a learned transform codec 101. Analysis and synthesis transforms run on the neural accelerator in 16-bit floating point; the hyperprior and context models that produce the entropy coder's probability tables run in integer arithmetic (as in section 2.10), and the entropy coder is a range coder with 16-bit integer cumulative distributions, so every device decodes identical symbols. Decoded pixels may differ slightly between devices (for example by at most 2 code values at 12 bits), but no later picture depends on them. Images are 12-bit, 4:4:4, in BT.2020 primaries with PQ, HLG or SDR transfer.

**3.2 Layers.** The codec produces progressive layers: layer 0 (102) is a preview of the whole picture, and each later layer 104, 106 and 108 refines the whole picture (for example by adding latent channels, then finer quantisation), so each arrival sharpens the entire image rather than revealing rows.

**3.3 Sizing to cover packets.** The layers are cut in order into chunks 110. The encoder sizes layer 0 to fit one chunk's payload (for example 1,990 bytes in a 2,024-byte chunk inside a 2,048-byte packet payload) and pads each later layer to end on a chunk boundary; in FIG. 4, layer 0 ends at chunk C0, layer 1 at C3, layer 2 at C7 and layer 3 at C11. A full-screen phone photo of about 3.16 million pixels has a design size of 15–40 KB (8 to 21 chunks), arriving in about 3 to 7 two-second ticks, with the preview in the first.

**3.4 Chunks in place of cover.** Each chunk 110 consists of a reference 111 and a ciphertext 113. The ciphertext is encrypted with a key derived from a per-image secret, the recipient's group leaf index and the chunk index, and the reference is derived the same way, so every chunk of every recipient has an unrelated reference and ciphertext. Storage nodes therefore cannot group chunks of one image or link the mailboxes of several recipients by matching items. On the time axis 112 of ticks, each tick provides a fixed number of outgoing packet slots 114. Chunks are stored to the recipient's mailbox in slots that would otherwise carry cover packets 118, framed exactly like message fragments 116 and of the same length, so the external traffic pattern does not change. The slots of a tick are filled in priority order: receipts and acknowledgements, message fragments 116, typing events, directory queries, then image chunks in layer order, round-robin among images so that one large image does not delay another image's preview; unused slots carry cover packets 118.

**3.5 Offer.** Before the chunks, the sender sends an end-to-end encrypted offer 120 carrying the per-image secret, the chunk count, the chunk index at which each layer completes, and the native size, and optionally the preview chunk C0 itself, so the preview appears as soon as the offer arrives. After each complete layer the receiver decodes and redraws: a preview 122 after layer 0, refined pictures 124 and 125 after layers 1 and 2, and the full picture 126 after the last layer.

**3.6 Live and spatial content.** A Live Photo is sent as the still image followed by up to 3 s of motion coded as a video residual predicted from the still, with constant-bitrate audio, for a design size of about 200 KB, delivered in the same ticks; the receiver reconstructs a platform Live Photo with a newly generated asset identifier. A second eye of a spatial photo is coded as a residual from the first.

**3.7 Metadata.** The image format has no field for capture metadata (EXIF, XMP, location, device identifiers, capture time, file names); orientation is applied to the pixels, and only colour and HDR metadata are carried.

### 4. Native-resolution tiled delivery with viewport fetch (FIG. 5)

**4.1 Native resolution.** For stored media the sender never scales the source down to fit the codec, link or budget; only the bitrate changes. Lower resolutions are a receiver choice, by fetching fewer tiles or an overview track. (The reduced coded size of section 2.8 is a per-tier choice for live sessions, with the native size signalled and restored by the receiver.)

**4.2 Tiles.** Referring to FIG. 5, a frame 130 larger than a hardware decoder's single-picture limit (for example the HEVC level 5.1 limits of 8,912,896 luma samples and 8,444 samples per dimension) is coded as a uniform grid of tiles 132, each of a width and height that are multiples of 128 from 512 to 2,048 pixels, with remainder tiles 144 in the last column and row of at least 64 pixels. Each tile is an independent elementary stream 148 with its own parameter sets and reference pictures and no prediction across tile edges, so a decoder holds only the tiles it decodes. Frames up to 43,008 × 21,504 pixels (a 42K equirectangular frame) are supported, giving at most 3,528 tiles at 512 pixels.

**4.3 Overview and index.** A stream has a stream header 141, and each segment begins at a random-access frame and carries a segment index 142 of each tile's byte range. A stream may also include an overview track 140: the whole frame at reduced size (for example 4,096 × 2,048 for a full sphere), as an additional representation that does not replace the native tiles.

**4.4 Viewport fetch.** For 360° and VR content, the receiver follows a fetch order 146: first the stream header 141, the segment indexes 142 and the overview track 140; then the tiles in its viewport 134; then neighbouring tiles 136; then the remaining tiles 138. When the stream is stored as transfer chunks (section 6), every fetch 149 is a request for a fixed-size chunk by hash through an anonymity network, so nodes cannot tell which tiles, or which part of the scene, are being fetched. For live sources over relays, each tile is a separate constant-rate track, and a viewer keeps a fixed number of tile subscriptions and moves them as its view changes rather than adding and removing subscriptions.

**4.5 Ingest.** For cameras that produce 11K to 18K video, the encoder runs on the camera operator's own computer, which takes the camera feed and sends the tiled format; nothing is transcoded on network servers.

### 5. Receiver frame interpolation to high display rates at no extra bits (FIG. 6)

**5.1 Timestamps.** Every frame carries a presentation timestamp on a 1 MHz clock 150 counted from the stream start, with no frame-rate field and no wall-clock time. Coded frames within a track are at least 500 µs apart (minimum spacing 158), allowing coded rates up to 2,000 fps, and the rate may change at any frame (for example at the frame 160 in FIG. 6, where the coded rate changes from 120 to 60 fps).

**5.2 Interpolation.** When the display's rate exceeds the coded rate, the receiver's neural accelerator interpolates frames 154 between consecutive coded frames 152 and times them on the same clock to the display refresh instants 156, for example from 60 fps coded to 120 fps on a 120 Hz display (one interpolated frame between coded frames), 240 fps where enabled (three), and up to 2,000 fps on high-refresh displays. Interpolated frames cost no transmitted bits, so a 120 or 240 fps call uses the bitrate of a 60 fps call.

**5.3 No frame loss.** The receiver presents every coded frame 152 it decodes and only adds or skips interpolated frames 154 to match the display. Coded frames may be stored, exported and counted as received (162); interpolated frames are for display only (164) and are never stored, exported or counted as received frames, and a player that captures or exports a frame indicates whether it was interpolated.

**5.4 Native high rate.** Stored high-rate sources (for example 4K at 120 fps or 240 fps slow motion) are coded at their native rate, and interpolation applies only above it.

### 6. Private chunked transfer with optional pre-send transcode (FIG. 7, FIG. 8A, FIG. 8B)

**6.1 Mode.** Referring to FIG. 7, for each source file 170 the sending device presents a mode choice 172 with the sizes and estimated times of each mode: a bit-exact mode that sends the original bytes, and, for media, a transcode mode 172b in which the sender's own device transcodes the source to the constant-bitrate format at the source's native resolution, frame rate and dynamic range, at a standard or visually lossless quality. The transcode never runs on network servers and never without the user's choice. The result is the payload 174, whose hash (for example SHA-512) is recorded. For example, a 4K ProRes 422 HQ file measured about 450 Mbit/s in prototype tests (Example 2 source), while the format's 4K HDR design value is 9.6 Mbit/s at streaming quality.

**6.2 Keys.** The sender draws a random 32-byte transfer secret and derives a distinct key for each chunk, each manifest chunk and a root chunk, and a distinct deletion secret for each, with HKDF-SHA-512 and domain-separation labels. Each key encrypts exactly one plaintext with AES-256-GCM. No convergent encryption is used, so the same file sent twice yields unrelated chunks.

**6.3 Chunks.** The payload 174 is cut (176) into fixed pieces (for example 61,420 bytes plus a 4-byte length, sealed to exactly 61,440 bytes so a storage request fits a 65,536-byte packet payload). If the payload needs n data chunks, the sender pads the count (178) to max(4, Padmé(n)) data chunks, padding with empty chunks, which leaks only on the order of log log n bits of size. Data chunks are grouped (180) into stripes of k data and m parity chunks (for example 16 and 4) using systematic Reed–Solomon coding over GF(2^8) applied before encryption, and every data and parity chunk is sealed separately (182) with its own key, so they are indistinguishable.

**6.4 Addressing and storage.** Each sealed chunk is addressed (184) by a hash of its ciphertext. The chunks are stored (186) on storage nodes 40 chosen by rendezvous hashing of the address among independent operators (for example three replicas); the storage nodes verify on storage that the address matches the content and that the length is exact. With each chunk the sender stores the hash of its deletion secret, so only the sender (or whoever it gives the secret) can delete it. Retention is bounded.

**6.5 Manifest.** An encrypted manifest 188 lists the transfer identifier, the mode, the file name (inside the encryption only, and optional), the media type, the payload size and hash, in transcode mode optionally the size and hash of the original, the coding parameters, and the address of every chunk. It is signed with a post-quantum signature (for example ML-DSA-87); in a group conversation the signature key is the sender's rotating group signature key, whose private half is later revealed to the group, so the manifest is as deniable as a message. The manifest is stored as chunks whose addresses are listed in a root chunk 189.

**6.6 Offer and pull.** The sender delivers an offer 190 carrying the transfer secret, transfer identifier and root address to the recipient inside an end-to-end encrypted channel (for example an MLS group with a post-quantum cipher suite) or sealed to the recipient's ML-KEM-1024 key. The recipient fetches the root and manifest, verifies the signature and consistency, then pulls (192) chunks by address in an order of its choosing (for example viewport tiles first), through single-use reply blocks of an anonymity network, several per request, carried in constant streams 38. It decodes (194) each stripe as soon as any k of its chunks are held, verifies (196) the payload hash before presenting or saving anything, and presents or saves the payload and sends a done message (198), after which the sender deletes the chunks. To keep fetch volume tied to the padded size, the recipient may continue requesting chunks up to the stripe's total.

**6.7 Constant carriers.** All storage and fetch requests travel only in constant streams 38 whose schedule does not depend on whether a transfer is queued: the user's own node 39 at its constant rate; the phone's Wi-Fi-and-charging window, which opens whenever its conditions hold, after a random delay, whether or not anything is queued; or, at the user's choice, a two-relay session at a call tier when both sides are online.

**6.8 Sender states (FIG. 8A).** The sender moves from an idle state 200 to a preparing state 202 (transcode, hash, cut, pad, erasure code and seal every chunk, and write all sealed chunks and the manifest to a durable outbox), then to an uploading state 204 (store each chunk inside constant-stream slots, root and manifest first), a confirming state 206 (check storage in batches; a chunk is stored when two of three replicas report it; re-store the rest), an offered state 208 (send the offer if not yet sent) and a complete state 210 when every recipient device has reported done, after which the chunks are deleted by default. A preparing error leads to a failed state 218; retention passing in the offered state leads to an expired state 212; a user cancel from any state leads to a cancelling state 214, in which every chunk is deleted by its secret, and then to a cancelled state 216.

**6.9 Recipient states (FIG. 8B).** The recipient moves from an offered state 220 (showing sender, padded size and preview, with automatic acceptance below a user-set size) to a fetch-root state 222, a fetch-manifest state 224, a verify-manifest state 226 (version, signature with the sender's key, chunk count and coding consistency, size limits), a fetching state 228 (requests in the recipient's chosen order; each reply checked against its address and decrypted; each stripe decoded as soon as k chunks are held), a verifying state 230 (payload hash, and per-frame sample hashes for lossless media) and a done state 232, in which it sends a done message. An invalid manifest, a stripe with too few reachable chunks, or a hash mismatch leads to a failed state 234; a user decline from any state leads to a discarded state 236.

**6.10 Resumption.** Both sides record every state and per-chunk status in a durable state store 238 before the network action it records. Storage, fetch and offer operations are idempotent by address or identifier, so either side can stop and resume at any time; a recipient that finds chunks expired requests the missing stripes and the sender re-stores them from its outbox.

**6.11 Performance.** Over the anonymity carriers the transfer is slower per byte than a direct link, because of packet headers, parity, padding and a fixed rate. A bit-exact send is not faster than a line-rate transfer protocol. The time saving arises when the transcode mode reduces the bytes moved.

### 7. Colour, HDR and metadata

Video uses BT.2020 primaries with PQ (SMPTE ST 2084) or HLG (ITU-R BT.2100) transfer at 10 bits (12 via the residual layer); images use 12 bits at 4:4:4. Mastering display metadata, content light levels and per-scene tone-mapping curves are carried; the receiver tone-maps to the display's current headroom. Capture metadata has no field in the format, and in-band codec messages are restricted to colour, HDR, picture hash and recovery-point messages.

### 8. Examples (prototype measurements)

The following were measured on prototype software. The base layer was encoded by the hardware HEVC encoder of a current phone (Apple iPhone Air, iOS 26.6.1) with live-call settings at the stated target bitrates; the enhancement was the phone's general-purpose low-latency super-resolution scaler on its Neural Engine (2× per pass), not networks trained as in section 2.7; and quality was scored on a computer with VMAF (model v0.6.1, and the 4K model for 4K sources) against the source, with any frame the encoder dropped scored as the previous frame held. Comparison codecs were the same phone's hardware H.264 and HEVC encoders at full size with the same settings. Bitrates are the actual average bitrates of the coded streams.

**Example 1 (720 × 1280 short-form video, 30 fps, 44.7 s, 1,341 frames).** At a 500 kbit/s target, a 360 × 640 base coded at 351.2 kbit/s and enlarged by one super-resolution pass to 720 × 1280 (about 359 passes per second on the Neural Engine) scored VMAF 59.15. The same base with a Lanczos upscale only scored 54.97. Full-size hardware HEVC at 351.2 kbit/s scored 53.17, and full-size hardware H.264 at 350.8 kbit/s scored 30.6. At a 250 kbit/s target the reduced base with super resolution scored 41.13 at 176.3 kbit/s against 32.64 for full-size HEVC at 176.4 kbit/s; at a 1,000 kbit/s target, 74.97 at 700.8 kbit/s against 73.49 at 700.6 kbit/s. At a 2,000 kbit/s target the full-size base scored highest (87.48 at 1,399.6 kbit/s), which is why the coded size is chosen per tier (section 2.8). At a 64 kbit/s target the full-size HEVC encoder dropped 697 of 1,341 frames, while a 180 × 320 base with two super-resolution passes kept every frame (VMAF 14.71 at 46.3 kbit/s against 18.33 for HEVC with the dropped frames held). For reference, a software AV1 encoder (SVT-AV1, real-time preset, on a desktop computer; the phone has no AV1 encoder) scored 66.88 at 517.8 kbit/s.

**Example 2 (3840 × 2160 video, 29.97 fps, 6 s, 180 frames).** At a 4,000 kbit/s target, a 1920 × 1080 base coded at 2,174.5 kbit/s and upscaled to 3840 × 2160 (by Lanczos, because the phone's scaler did not accept 1920 × 1080 input in this test) scored VMAF 62.55, against 64.58 for full-size hardware HEVC at 2,797.8 kbit/s and 57.11 for full-size hardware H.264 at 2,777.2 kbit/s: about 22% fewer bits than HEVC for about 2 points lower, and higher quality than H.264 at fewer bits. At a 500 kbit/s target, a 960 × 540 base at 353.2 kbit/s with one super-resolution pass to 1920 × 1080 (about 204 passes per second) and a Lanczos step to 3840 × 2160 scored 45.52, against 37.79 for full-size HEVC at 369.9 kbit/s and 36.73 for H.264, which could not go below 815.6 kbit/s. The phone encoded the half-size base at about 97 frames per second against about 49 for the full-size HEVC encode.

**Example 3 (display above the native size at the same bits).** The 720 × 1280 streams of Example 1 at 1,399.6, 351.2 and 46.3 kbit/s were presented by the receiver at 2160 × 3840 (by Lanczos in this run) with no change to the transmitted stream, and scored VMAF 87.09, 59.2 and 14.69 against the source, compared with 87.48, 59.15 and 14.71 for the same streams at 720 × 1280: nine times the displayed pixels at no added bits and unchanged measured fidelity.

## Abstract

A sender fixes the bits on the wire and the receiver improves the picture. A hardware video base layer is coded at constant bitrate, split into fixed-size encrypted objects sent at a fixed rate with indistinguishable padding, and the receiver's neural accelerator applies super resolution, artefact removal and frame interpolation that it selects for its own hardware, outside the prediction loop, at no bit cost and without drift. Images are coded by a learned codec into progressive layers sized to the fixed packets of an anonymity network's cover traffic, so a preview arrives in the first packet. Video stays at native resolution in independent tiles up to 43,008 × 21,504 pixels with viewport-first fetching. Files move as padded, erasure-coded, end-to-end sealed chunks addressed by ciphertext hash and pulled by the recipient, optionally after a native-resolution transcode.
